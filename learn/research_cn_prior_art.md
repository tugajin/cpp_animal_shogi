# Conspiracy Number Search — Prior Art for a UBFM + Neural-Network Experiment

## Summary

**Experiment under review.** In a best-first minimax search (UBFM-style) whose leaves are evaluated by a neural network, (a) replace the usual "expand the most promising leaf" rule with conspiracy-number-based leaf selection — expand leaves belonging to a minimal conspiracy that could change the root's win/draw/loss category (threshold ±0.2 on the backed-up value) — and (b) record the root's conspiracy number as a confidence annotation on self-play training records.

**Bottom line.** Both halves have ancestors, but the *combination* was not found in the literature searched:

- Conspiracy-number leaf selection itself is the original 1988 idea, and it has been revived in recognisably modern form — Pawlewicz & Hayward's Sibling CNS (2015/2016) uses exactly a root interval $[v_{min},v_{max}]$ around the current minimax value and expands a most-proving/conspiring leaf. A ±threshold version of the experiment is therefore an incremental variant of CNS/SCNS, not a new selection principle.
- UBFM + a learned (neural) leaf evaluator + self-play is Athénan / Descent (Cohen-Solal; Cohen-Solal & Cazenave, arXiv:2012.10700). That line of work, as read, contains **no conspiracy numbers at all** — its leaf rule is "expand a leaf on a best (root-valued) path," and its robustness-flavoured addition is *safe decision* at move-choice time, not conspiracy-based expansion.
- Using a conspiracy number as a **per-position score attached to a position** also has precedent — as a critical-position identifier (Khalid et al., 2015), as a long-term position evaluation alongside the evaluation function (Single Conspiracy Number, Zhang & Iida, 2018), and as a position-difficulty measure in chess (Lorenz). 
- **Negative result (Q5):** within the search scope described in §5, **no work was found that records a conspiracy number (or any equivalent search-tree robustness measure) as a confidence/reliability annotation or weight on self-play reinforcement-learning training records** in an AlphaZero-style pipeline. The closest inverse-direction work is the Proof Cost Network (Wu et al., ICLR 2022), which trains a network to *predict* a proof-number-derived cost and uses that prediction as a solver heuristic — it does not annotate or weight playing-strength training data by a robustness score.

Confidence: high for the classical CNS/PNS genealogy and for the Athénan facts (primary papers opened); medium for some 2015–2021 JAIST results, several of which rest on abstracts/secondary summaries in the index rather than full texts opened live.

## Findings

### 1. Original conspiracy number search (McAllester, 1988) and main variants

**Definition used throughout.** For a node/root and a target value $v$, $cn(root,v)$ is the least number of leaves ("conspirators") whose evaluations must change to $v$ for the root's minimax value to become $v$; $cn=0$ for the current value and $\infty$ for proved terminal values. In "conspiracy theory" form, $C^{<}[T]$ / $C^{>}[T]$ count the leaves that must change to move the root value down/up by a singular margin $\delta$, and are explicitly described as expressing *confidence* that a bound will hold under further expansion. Sources: Chess Programming Wiki, *Conspiracy Numbers*, https://chessprogramming.org/Conspiracy_Numbers (index, read 2026-11-20); Pawlewicz & Hayward 2016, full text opened, https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~hayward/papers/cnsrss.pdf (verified live, read 2026-11-20).

#### 1.1 McAllester (1988) — Conspiracy Number Search (CNS)

- **Authors / year / venue:** David A. McAllester, 1988, *Artificial Intelligence* 35(3):287–310, "Conspiracy Numbers for Min-Max Search" (preceded by a 1985 MIT AI Lab technical report, "A New Procedure for Growing Minimax Trees").
- **Method (2–3 sentences):** Motivated by the observation that an alpha-beta decision can rest on a single leaf evaluation, so one erroneous leaf can be disastrous, CNS is a best-first search that grows the tree in memory — selection, expansion/evaluation, backup — so as to maximise the conspiracy required to change the root value. It maintains a range of possible root values and keeps expanding a conspirator of the most plausible alternative value until the range width falls below $W$ and the conspiracy numbers of the remaining alternatives exceed a threshold $T$.
- **Key empirical result:** The 1988 paper is primarily conceptual/algorithmic; the convincing *empirical* verdicts came from followers (below) and were mixed-to-negative for plain CNS in chess. The Chess Programming Wiki summary notes two structural costs already visible in early implementations: because $W$ depends on the number of distinct evaluation values, the fine-grained evaluation needed for positional play makes CNS inefficient, and a final alpha-beta quiescence search was expensive for lack of bounds.
- **Relation to the experiment:** This *is* the experiment's leaf-selection idea, in threshold form ($W$, $T$) rather than win/draw/loss-category form. The proposed ±0.2 category thresholds are a coarsening of McAllester's value range — which, per the weakness above, is precisely the direction (fewer distinct values) that makes CNS tractable.
- Sources: https://chessprogramming.org/Conspiracy_Number_Search (opened, verified live, 2026-11-20); https://chessprogramming.org/Conspiracy_Numbers (index).

#### 1.2 Schaeffer (1990); Klingbeil & Schaeffer (1988/1990); van der Meulen (1990); Lister & Schaeffer (1994) — first empirical wave

- **Authors / year / venue:** Jonathan Schaeffer, 1990, *Artificial Intelligence* 43(1):67–84, "Conspiracy Numbers" (also *Advances in Computer Chess 5*, 1989); Norbert Klingbeil & Jonathan Schaeffer, 1990, *Computational Intelligence* 6:1–11, "Empirical Results with Conspiracy Numbers"; Maarten van der Meulen, 1990, *ICCA Journal* 13(1), "Conspiracy-Number Search"; Lisa Lister & Jonathan Schaeffer, 1994, *Computers & Mathematics with Applications* 27(1), "An Analysis of the Conspiracy Numbers Algorithm."
- **Method:** Schaeffer recast McAllester's definition for practical chess search (non-terminal leaves $cn=1$, terminals $\infty$); Klingbeil & Schaeffer experimented on random and application-generated trees with search-strategy variants (notably more breadth); van der Meulen implemented CNS (including a parallel version, 1988, on a 10-processor system) in the chess testbed *Arachne*; Lister & Schaeffer analysed tree growth as a function of the conspiracy threshold $CT$.
- **Key empirical results:** Klingbeil & Schaeffer's abstract: more emphasis on breadth over depth gives significant improvements, and their enhancements "are capable of solving 41% more problems than McAllester's original proposal." Van der Meulen's verdict was harsher: experiments with cn-search as published by McAllester in Arachne were "not encouraging," prompting a revised "applied cn-search." Lister & Schaeffer showed McAllester's original builds a tree of best-case size $O(w^{CT}/(w-1))$ ($w$ = branching factor) and that an improvement reduces this to $O(CT^2)$ — i.e., the original's growth in the threshold is exponential.
- **Relation to the experiment:** This wave establishes the standing empirical prior: naive CNS underperforms in a real game (chess) unless the selection strategy is modified. Any positive result for the proposed variant will need to be read against van der Meulen's negative chess result, and breadth/threshold tuning should be expected to matter.
- Sources: Crossref abstract, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111%2FJ.1467-8640.1990.TB00125.X (index); https://chessprogramming.org/Arachne (index); https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0898122194900051 (index); all read 2026-11-20.

#### 1.3 McAllester & Yuret (1993/2002) — Alpha-Beta Conspiracy Search (ABC)

- **Authors / year / venue:** David McAllester & Deniz Yuret, draft 1993; *ICGA Journal* 25(1):16–35, 2002, "Alpha-Beta-Conspiracy Search."
- **Method:** Instead of a best-first CNS, ABC keeps depth-first alpha-beta and gives each node **two depths**, one per player/strategy: a max-strategy establishes a lower bound and cares only about the forcedness of max moves, a min-strategy an upper bound and only about min moves. The authors state the two-depth procedure is formally derived from conspiracy theory and justify it with two theorems relating ABC search to conspiracy numbers. In effect, "depth" becomes a per-side conspiracy/forcedness budget (e.g., counting moves that have reached $\alpha-\delta$) rather than plies.
- **Key empirical result:** No controlled head-to-head Elo/tactical-suite victory over alpha-beta is reported in the paper's abstract as indexed. Its practical footprint is by adoption: Yuret's CV states the world-champion program *Socrates* "used some of our techniques successfully," and McAllester's own page states conspiracy numbers and ABC "influenced the design of Deep Blue" and were "mentioned by the Deep Blue team" — both are author-side claims, not independent evaluations, and should be cited as such.
- **Relation to the experiment:** ABC is the main precedent for importing conspiracy reasoning into a *conventional* search rather than replacing the search — analogous in spirit to importing conspiracy selection into UBFM. It also warns that the import may end up operating on move-choice/depth allocation rather than on leaf selection per se.
- Sources: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/ICG-2002-25103 (index abstract); https://www.icga.org/icga/journal/contents/content25-1.htm (index); https://home.ttic.edu/~dmcallester/chess.html (index, author claim); https://www2.denizyuret.com/yuretcv.html (index, author CV); read 2026-11-20.

#### 1.4 Lorenz, Rottmann et al. (1995–2002) — Controlled CNS (CCNS), Conspiracy-2, Parallel CCNS

- **Authors / year / venue:** Ulf Lorenz, Valentin Rottmann, Rainer Feldmann & Peter Mysliwietz, 1995, *ICCA Journal* 18(3):135–147, "Controlled Conspiracy-Number Search"; Lorenz & Rottmann, 1996, *Advances in Computer Chess 8* (parallel version); Lorenz, 2000, "Controlled Conspiracy-2 Search," *STACS 2000* (LNCS 1770) and Paderborn dissertation; Lorenz, 2002, "Parallel Controlled Conspiracy Number Search," *Euro-Par 2002* (LNCS 2400).
- **Method:** CCNS fixes plain CNS's uncontrolled growth by passing **CN targets** (security demands) top-down: each node is told what conspiracy number it must achieve, targets are split over successors, a *set* of leaves is selected in one action, and "extended conspiracy numbers" are defined with respect to changing the root *decision* (move) rather than the root value. Conspiracy-2 (Cc2s) specialises this to envelopes whose root decision is stable against a *single* faulty leaf evaluation, with an efficiency analysis.
- **Key empirical results:** The strongest positive results in the classical CNS literature. The ICCA abstract states CCNS was "stronger than the alpha-beta algorithm in tactical positions" on a recognised test set and played "reasonably even in non-tactical positions"; its program *Ulysses* solved 281 of 300 Win-at-Chess positions at 300 s/position and was described by its authors as the first CNS-based program with an acceptable computer-chess tournament result. The parallel version in *P.ConNerS* is reported (Lorenz, Euro-Par 2002) as the first program to win a (FIDE) grandmaster tournament, with speedup ≈50 on 159 processors, and won the 8th International Paderborn Computer-Chess Championship 1999 (Cc2s version).
- **Relation to the experiment:** CCNS is the closest classical analogue to the experiment's *category* formulation: extended conspiracy numbers target a change of **decision**, and the proposed thresholds target a change of **win/draw/loss category** — a coarser decision. It is also the main evidence that CNS-family search can beat alpha-beta, but only tactically and only after substantial control machinery (targets, sets of leaves, quiescence with windows) that a minimal UBFM variant would lack.
- Sources: https://www.icga.org/icga/journal/contents/node14.html (index abstract); https://chessprogramming.org/Ulysses (index); https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/3-540-45706-2_57.pdf (index); https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/3-540-46541-3_39 (index); read 2026-11-20.

#### 1.5 Elkan (1989) — conspiracy numbers outside minimax games

- **Author / year / venue:** Charles Elkan, 1989, *IJCAI-89*, pp. 341–347, "Conspiracy Numbers and Caching for Searching And/Or Trees and Theorem-Proving."
- **Method / result:** Transplants conspiracy numbers to AND/OR-tree search (an AO*-style best-first algorithm obeying a "sunk costs" principle) and to a depth-first iterative-deepening SLD-resolution theorem prover with caching of successes *and* failures, including matching cached goals up to substitution. Experimental results indicated conspiracy numbers and especially the caching scheme were effective in practice.
- **Relation to the experiment:** Minor, but it is the earliest demonstration that the conspiracy idea is a generic "how many leaves must flip" heuristic separable from minimax play — the same separability the experiment relies on when treating CN as an annotation.
- Source: https://ijcai.org/proceedings/1989/54 (index, read 2026-11-20).

#### 1.6 "Probabilistic conspiracy numbers (PCN)" — a terminology caution

No canonical algorithm under the exact name *"Probabilistic Conspiracy Numbers"* / PCN was found: exact-phrase searches returned no such work (only unrelated conspiracy-theory material). The probabilistic treatments that *do* exist under other names are:

1. **Shperberg, Shimony & Felner (2017)** — see §4.3: §4.1 "High-BVPI sets: Using Conspiracies" explicitly *defines a probabilistic variant of conspiracy numbers* inside an MCTS value-of-information method. This is the closest documented match to the mission's phrase.
2. **Vu et al. (2016)** — see §4.2: treats the conspiracy number of a situation as (proportional to) a probability distribution over evaluations.
3. **Probability-based Proof Number Search (PPNS) / Probability-based Proof Number (PPN)** (Zhang's JAIST work; Primanita, Khalid & Iida, 2021) — the probabilistic descendant on the *proof-number* side, using information from explored and unexplored nodes; reported to cut nodes explored by up to 57% vs. PNS in Connect Four/Othello experiments (secondary summary).
4. **Not to be confused with:** Wu et al.'s **PCN = Proof Cost Network** (ICLR 2022, §3.4) — same acronym, proof-number lineage, no conspiracy numbers.

**Known weaknesses of the (non-probabilistic) core idea, consolidated from the sources above:** (i) all leaf changes are counted equally — a conspiracy of size $k$ is treated as equally (un)likely regardless of *how far* each leaf's value must move or how probable such an error is; this is exactly the gap the probabilistic variants in (1)–(3) were introduced to close, and a neural evaluator makes it sharper, because leaf errors are correlated and magnitude-dependent; (ii) best-first memory cost — the whole tree is kept in memory, deep and narrow; (iii) exponential sensitivity to the threshold in the original formulation (Lister & Schaeffer); (iv) fine-grained evaluations blow up the value range $W$ and destroy efficiency (Chess Programming Wiki summary), forcing either coarse categories — as the experiment proposes — or interval/function representations (SCNS, §4.1); (v) overhead of maintaining conspiracy numbers/functions at every node for every candidate value.

### 2. Relationship to proof-number search (PNS, PN², df-pn)

#### 2.1 How they differ

| | Conspiracy Number Search | Proof-Number Search |
|---|---|---|
| Tree / values | Minimax tree; leaves have a heuristic evaluation of *any* value | AND/OR tree; leaves are true / false / unknown (win / not-win) |
| Numbers | $cn(n,v)$ for *every* target value $v$ (a function of $v$ in modern form) | Two scalars per node: proof number $pn$ (min leaves to prove) and disproof number $dn$ (min leaves to disprove); OR node: $pn=\min$, $dn=\sum$; AND node: $pn=\sum$, $dn=\min$ |
| Goal / stopping | Stop when alternatives are *unlikely*: their conspiracy numbers exceed a threshold and the value range is narrow — a confidence criterion, no proof | Stop when $pn(root)=0$ (proved) or $dn(root)=0$ (disproved) — an exact game-theoretic result |
| Leaf selection | Expand a conspirator of a plausible alternative value | Expand a *most proving node* (MPN): a leaf whose proof lowers root $pn$ and whose disproof lowers root $dn$ |
| Evaluation function | Essential | None in pure form — only solved positions are scored (explicit contrast in Saffidine & Cazenave 2012: CNS "tries to eliminate unlikely values of the evaluation function," MOPNS/PNS "tries to prove the outcome that costs the less effort") |

Genealogy: PNS is explicitly a *specialisation* of conspiracy numbers to binary AND/OR search — Allis first used proof/disproof numbers, still called conspiracy numbers, while solving Connect Four. Conversely, Pawlewicz & Hayward present CNS as a *generalisation* of PNS obtained by letting leaf scores be any real value and replacing $(pn,dn)$ scalars by $(pn,dn)$ *functions* of the target value, with $CN_n(v)=pn(v)+dn(v)$. Sources: https://hal.science/hal-01614783v1/document (index); https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~hayward/papers/cnsrss.pdf (opened); https://chessprogramming.org/Proof-Number_Search (index); read 2026-11-20.

**Relevance to the experiment's ±0.2 win/draw/loss thresholds:** with only three categories, the proposed conspiracy number is much closer to the PNS end of the spectrum than to full CNS — for each category boundary it is essentially a proof/disproof-style count. MOPNS/GPNS (Saffidine & Cazenave, ECAI 2012; *Computers and Games*) is therefore directly relevant prior art for handling *multiple outcomes* with proof numbers in one search instead of repeated binary searches, reporting fewer node creations on Connect Four and Woodpush than cumulative repeated PNS.

#### 2.2 The works

- **Allis, van der Meulen & van den Herik (1994)** — "Proof-Number Search," *Artificial Intelligence* 66:91–124; and Allis's 1994 Maastricht thesis *Searching for Solutions in Games and Artificial Intelligence*. Best-first AND/OR search expanding the most proving node. Used in solving Connect Four, Qubic (4×4×4) and Gomoku — games Allis is credited with solving. **Relation:** the proof that a conspiracy-derived count can *solve* games when the target is binary/exact — the regime the experiment's category thresholds approximate, but with heuristic (neural) leaves there is no proof, only confidence.
- **Breuker (1998) / Allis (1994) — PN².** A second, limited PNS is run at the leaves of the main PNS to evaluate them, giving the main search far more information for the same memory, at extra compute cost; the second tree is discarded. Later used by PN² solvers for Fanorona and 6×6 Lines of Action. **Relation:** a two-level remedy for PNS's memory problem — the analogue problem (maintaining CN functions in a growing UBFM tree) is unaddressed in the CNS literature.
- **Seo (1995) — PN\*; Nagai (1998/1999/2002) — PDS and df-pn.** Seo's PN* is the first depth-first, iterative-deepening version of PNS, using the proof number as a threshold plus a transposition table and multiple iterative deepening; Nagai's df-pn extends this to both $pn$ and $dn$ thresholds, is proved to select the same most-proving nodes as best-first PNS, and stores nodes only in a transposition table, largely removing the memory bottleneck. **Relation:** df-pn is the template for making a best-first conspiracy/proof method memory-feasible — relevant if the experiment's in-memory UBFM tree becomes the bottleneck.
- **Where PNS actually succeeded:** overwhelmingly as a *solver*, not a general player — tsume-shogi (Seo's PN* solver solved the 1525-ply *Microcosmos*; df-pn solvers solved almost all 300+-ply problems), checkers (proof components in Schaeffer et al.'s 2007 weak solution), Go life-and-death/tactical problems, Hex solvers, Connect6 endgames, chess mate problems (e.g., the *Prover* program), and antichess/losing-chess modes. The standard survey verdict (Winands, "Proof-Number Search and its Variants"): PN search has been successfully applied to the endgames of Awari, Chess, Checkers, Shogi and Go, and outperforms alpha-beta *in proving the game-theoretic value of endgame positions*, while preferring slim over wide subtrees. Pawlewicz & Hayward add the complementary warning: as a *player* PNS "can be weak, especially early in games with search trees with almost uniform branching." Sources: https://dke.maastrichtuniversity.nl/m.winands/documents/pnchapter.pdf (index); https://www.academia.edu/101452912/Deep_df_pn_and_Its_Application_to_Connect6 (index); read 2026-11-20.
- **Relation (overall):** PNS's track record predicts where the experiment's rule should help most — sharp, forcing, near-terminal positions where few leaves decide the category — and where it should help least: quiet, uniform-branching middlegame positions where conspiracy counts are large and undiscriminating.

### 3. Conspiracy numbers + best-first minimax (UBFM / Descent) or neural-network leaf evaluation

#### 3.1 The UBFM baseline the experiment modifies

- **Korf & Chickering (1996)** — "Best-First Minimax Search," *Artificial Intelligence* 84(1–2):299–337. Unbounded Best-First Minimax iteratively expands a leaf that lies on a *best path* — a leaf whose backed-up value equals the root value. **Relation:** UBFM's rule already expands only leaves that currently *determine* the root value; the experiment's rule differs in expanding leaves that could *change the root's category*, i.e., conspirators for the nearest alternative category rather than supporters of the current value. That difference — defending the current value vs. attacking it at its weakest boundary — is the experiment's actual novelty claim, and it should be stated that way.
- Source: cited in https://arxiv.org/html/2012.10700v2 (opened) and https://arxiv.org/html/2603.24572v1 (index); read 2026-11-20.

#### 3.2 Athénan / Descent — UBFM with a neural evaluator, no conspiracy numbers

- **Authors / year / venue:** Quentin Cohen-Solal, 2020, "Learning to Play Two-Player Perfect-Information Games without Knowledge" (journal version *JMLR* 27, 2026; arXiv:2008.01188); Quentin Cohen-Solal & Tristan Cazenave, 2023, "Minimax Strikes Back," *Proc. AAMAS 2023*, pp. 1923–1931 (arXiv:2012.10700, submitted 19 Dec 2020, v2 5 May 2025).
- **Method:** Athénan learns a neural evaluation function $f_\theta$ by self-play reinforcement learning *without a policy head*. During learning it uses **Descent**, a UBFM modification that extends the best action sequence all the way to a terminal state each iteration (better propagation of endgame information); after learning it plays with Unbounded Minimax. Training uses *tree learning* — the network is updated toward the searched values of **all states encountered in the search tree**, not only states on the played path — plus reinforcement heuristics (quick wins/slow losses, scoring) and a completion technique for solved states.
- **Key empirical results:** Cohen-Solal (2020): Athénan beats ExIt (the prior state of the art for knowledge-free self-play) and surpasses state-of-the-art play in Hex, Othello and Arimaa with no prior knowledge; replacing Descent by plain UBFM, alpha-beta or MCTS gave worse results in those experiments. Cohen-Solal & Cazenave (2023): against Polygames (AlphaZero reimplementation), Athénan is competitive even when Polygames uses 100× more GPU on some games; generating training states costs ≈296× less, and with equal reasonable resources Athénan learns at least 7× faster without, and >30× faster with, a reinforcement heuristic.
- **Conspiracy numbers in this work: none found.** In arXiv:2012.10700 as read (related-work and algorithm sections), the search algorithms discussed are MCTS/PUCT, alpha-beta, UBFM, Descent, and "Unbounded Minimax with Safe Decision"; no conspiracy-number method, citation, or leaf rule appears. *Safe decision* ($UBFM_s$, Cohen-Solal 2025) is the nearest robustness-flavoured device: it performs the *same* UBFM search but chooses the final action differently — in the journal version, the action most frequently selected from the root during search rather than the action leading to the highest-valued state — and a broad 22-game comparison (Cohen-Solal 2025, arXiv:2505.09639) reports $UBFM_s$ best of all algorithms studied at short search time and best on average at medium time (17/22 games).
- **Relation to the experiment:** Athénan is simultaneously (a) the proof that the experiment's *substrate* (UBFM/Descent + neural leaves + self-play) is state-of-the-art-competitive, and (b) the gap: nobody in this line has replaced its best-path leaf rule with a conspiracy rule, nor annotated its tree-learning records with any per-state robustness score. Note also that Athénan's tree learning already trains on every tree state — a natural insertion point for a CN confidence weight, and a design the experiment should explicitly contrast with.
- Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.10700?context=cs (index); https://arxiv.org/html/2012.10700v2 (opened, verified live); https://arxiv.org/html/2008.01188v5 (index); https://s.bingheai.cn/pdf/2505.09639 (index); read 2026-11-20.

#### 3.3 Neural networks + proof numbers (adjacent, not conspiracy)

- **Gao, Cazenave et al. (Henderson-lineage) — Chao Gao et al., 2017,** "Focused Depth-first Proof Number Search using Convolutional Neural Networks for the Game of Hex," *IJCAI-17*, pp. 3660–3667 (as indexed, authors include work building on Henderson's FDFPN). FDFPN-CNN uses two CNNs trained from expert games: a value-approximation CNN to set the dynamic-widening size at a node, and a policy CNN to select promising children. On 8×8 Hex it performed "notably better" than FDFPN. **Relation:** the clearest precedent for *neural guidance inside a conspiracy-family search* — but for proof numbers and for *solving*, with supervised (expert) training, not self-play and not conspiracy numbers.
- Source: https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2017/0513.pdf (index, read 2026-11-20).

#### 3.4 Proof Cost Network — neural prediction of a proof-number-derived quantity

- **Authors / year / venue:** Ti-Rong Wu, Chung-Chin Shih, Ting Han Wei, Meng-Yu Tsai, Wei-Yuan Hsu & I-Chen Wu, 2022, "AlphaZero-based Proof Cost Network to Aid Game Solving," *ICLR 2022*.
- **Method:** Modify AlphaZero's training target so the network predicts *proof cost* — a heuristic estimating the work needed to solve a position (two targets: shortest solution path, and predicted solution-tree size) — instead of only win/loss. The resulting PCN is used as a heuristic inside MCTS-based and FDFPN solvers.
- **Key empirical result:** PCN heuristics solved more problems than AlphaZero networks on 15×15 Gomoku and 9×9 Killall-Go under the same limits, in both solver types.
- **Relation to the experiment:** The mirror image of the experiment's second half. Wu et al. *learn to predict* a search-effort/robustness quantity and feed it back into search; the experiment *computes* an exact (tree-relative) robustness quantity during search and attaches it to training records. It establishes that search-derived scalar annotations of positions are learnable and useful — supporting the plausibility of CN-as-annotation — while leaving CN itself, and confidence-weighting of ordinary self-play data, untouched.
- Sources: https://www.iclr.cc/virtual/2022/poster/7121 (index); https://openreview.net/references/pdf?id=KMqud-AXf (index); read 2026-11-20.

**Verdict for Q3:** No work was found that combines conspiracy-number leaf selection with UBFM/Descent, and none that combines conspiracy numbers with a neural-network leaf evaluator in any search. The two ingredients exist separately (CNS/SCNS selection; Athénan's neural UBFM), and the proof-number cousin has been combined with CNNs (FDFPN-CNN, PCN).

### 4. Recent work (2015–2025) reviving or extending conspiracy numbers

#### 4.1 Pawlewicz & Hayward (2015/2016) — Sibling Conspiracy Number Search (SCNS) — *the* modern revival

- **Authors / year / venue:** Jakub Pawlewicz & Ryan B. Hayward, 2015, *SoCS 2015* ("Sibling Conspiracy Number Search"); journal version 2016, *Theoretical Computer Science* 644:127–142, "Conspiracy Number Search with Relative Sibling Scores"; feature/parallel study, *Advances in Computer Games 2015* (LNCS).
- **Method:** An anytime, best-first CNS that does not trust absolute leaf evaluations: for each node it uses *relative* scores of all children (a "local" heuristic — in Hex, the Shannon/Anshelevich electric-circuit voltage, reliable among siblings, erratic across the tree). It maintains $(pn,dn)$ *functions* of the target value, sets a search interval $[v_{min},v_{max}]$ around the root value (in one formulation $v_{root}\pm\delta$, in another all values whose root proof/disproof numbers are below caps $P_{max},D_{max}$), repeatedly selects and expands a most-proving/conspiring leaf for those interval endpoints, and thereby refines the interval, converging to PNS as values become ±∞.
- **Key empirical result:** Their 11×11 Hex bot *DeepHex* "widely outperforms" *Wolve* (alpha-beta, previous champion) at all time levels and "narrowly outperforms" *MoHex* (MCTS, reigning champion) once time reaches 4 min/move; a parallel version scaled well to 2 threads, less efficiently to 4/8. The multi-step leaf-initialisation process was the most critical feature.
- **Relation to the experiment:** **Closest prior art overall.** The experiment's rule is SCNS's rule with three changes: leaf values from a neural net instead of a local sibling heuristic, interval endpoints fixed by win/draw/loss category boundaries (±0.2) instead of $\delta$/$P_{max}$ caps, and a UBFM tree instead of a PNS-style tree. A credible evaluation must include an SCNS-style interval baseline, or reviewers will ask why not.
- Source: https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~hayward/papers/cnsrss.pdf (opened, verified live); http://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~hayward/papers/scnsfeature.pdf (index); read 2026-11-20.

#### 4.2 The JAIST / Iida school (2015–2021) — CN as an analytic per-position measure

- **Khalid, Yusof, Iida & Ishitobi (2015),** "Critical Position Identification in Games and Its Application to Speculative Play," *ICAART 2015*; revised version Khalid, Ang, Yusof, Iida & Ishitobi (2015), "Identifying Critical Positions Based on Conspiracy Numbers," in *Agents and Artificial Intelligence* (Springer, ICAART 2015 revised selected papers). Records max/min conspiracy numbers through Tic-Tac-Toe and Othello games and uses them (via ↑/↓-tactic operators in Tic-Tac-Toe, CN variance in Othello) to identify *critical positions* — moments where a strategy-changing/speculative move might be applied. Abstract verdict: "interesting results are obtained with convincing evidences but future works are still needed." **Relation:** first use of CN as a *per-position annotation* rather than a search controller — the direct ancestor of the experiment's second half, but the annotation drives speculative play/entertainment analysis, never training.
- **Vu, Ishitobi, Terrillon & Iida (2016),** "Using Conspiracy Numbers for Improving Move Selection in Minimax Game-Tree Search," *ICAART 2016*, pp. 400–406. Starts from the admission that "CNS was invented as a possible search algorithm but did not find much success" and instead uses the *whole* conspiracy-number profile of a position, interpreted as a probability distribution over evaluations (high CN = hard to achieve that value), to select moves; evaluated on transcripts of professional Othello (US 2015 National Open) in secondary summaries. **Relation:** precedent for treating CN probabilistically and for CN-guided *move choice* (cf. ABC/CCNS extended CN), not for CN-guided expansion in a learned-evaluator search.
- **Zhang (Song) & Iida (2018/2019) — Single Conspiracy Number (SCN),** "Using Single Conspiracy Number for Long Term Position Evaluation," *CG 2018 / ICGA Journal* 40(3):269–280 (journal 2019); Zhang's JAIST PhD thesis, 2019, "A New Paradigm of Game Tree Search Using Proof Numbers"; Busala, Zhang, Iida, Khalid & Yusof (2019), SCN analysis in Checkers. SCN collapses the full CN function to a *single* number: the difficulty of the root's minimax value crossing one threshold — explicitly to fix full CN's computing-complexity and storage cost, and to be droppable into other frameworks (their testbed modifies the alpha-beta Chinese-chess engine *Xiangqi Wizard* to emit SCNs during search). On tactical, drawn and opening Chinese-chess positions, SCN was "more consistent and accurate for long-term position evaluation than the conventional way using evaluation function values only," and SCN + evaluation together best revealed game-progress patterns; in Checkers, SCN stabilised as depth increased. **Relation:** **the closest precedent for the confidence annotation** — a single threshold-crossing conspiracy count, computed inside an existing search, attached to a position alongside its evaluation. Differences from the experiment: SCN is used for *analysis/visualisation* of game progress, not as a training-data confidence/reliability field, and the evaluator is a classical alpha-beta evaluation, not a self-play-trained network. Note the experiment's ±0.2 category CN is, in SCN terms, two SCNs (one per boundary) or their minimum.
- **Primanita, Khalid & Iida (2021),** "Computing Games: Bridging the Gap Between Search and Entertainment," *IEEE Access* (DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2021.3079356). Applies PPNS and SCN across Chess, Chinese Chess, Connect Four, Othello and 2048, concluding PPN-based search reflects information quality / human-like intuition while SCN-based search reflects the player's experience/risk; JAIST's KAKEN summaries (2019–2022) continue this programme. **Relation:** shows the JAIST line was still active into the 2020s, but oriented to game-refinement/entertainment theory, not playing strength or RL.
- Sources: https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-27947-3_6 (index); https://dblp.org/pid/183/3802.rss / DOI 10.5220/0005753004000406 (index); JAIST thesis via https://dspace02.jaist.ac.jp/dspace/bitstream/10119/16064/2/paper.pdf (index excerpts; direct open failed — see Could not verify); https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/804262 (index); https://kaken.nii.ac.jp/grant/KAKENHI-PROJECT-17K19968/ (index); read 2026-11-20.

#### 4.3 Shperberg, Shimony & Felner (2017) — conspiracy numbers inside MCTS

- **Authors / year / venue:** Shahaf S. Shperberg, Solomon Eyal Shimony & Ariel Felner, 2017, "Monte-Carlo Tree Search using Batch Value of Perfect Information," *Proc. UAI 2017* (PMLR 15).
- **Method:** Defines Batch Value of Perfect Information (BVPI) for the MCTS selection phase — which *sets* of fringe nodes are worth sampling together — shows exact BVPI is NP-hard but polynomially approximable, and, in §4.1 "High-BVPI sets: Using Conspiracies," adapts McAllester's scheme by **defining a probabilistic variant of conspiracy numbers** to quickly detect minimal-conspiracy leaf sets with high BVPI. Empirical claim (abstract): the BVPI methods "outperform existing node-selection methods for MCTS in different scenarios," both standalone and inside a full MCTS.
- **Relation to the experiment:** The only located work that (a) is explicitly probabilistic-conspiracy, and (b) puts conspiracy sets inside a *modern* (MCTS) search as a batch leaf-selection rule — conceptually parallel to selecting a minimal conspiracy in UBFM, but driven by value-of-information under leaf-value distributions, with rollouts rather than neural evaluations, and with no training-data component.
- Source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mlresearch/r15/main/assets/shperberg17a/shperberg17a.pdf (index, read 2026-11-20).

#### 4.4 Japanese-language research (共謀数), GPW / IPSJ

- **Seo Masahiro (脊尾昌宏), 1995,** "共謀数を応用した詰め将棋プログラムについて" / "Application of Conspiracy Number to Tsume-Shogi Solver," *2nd Game Programming Workshop (GPW'95)*, Hakone, 23–25 Sept 1995; developed in his 1995 University of Tokyo M.Sc. thesis (the $C^*$ algorithm) and the 1998 book chapter "共謀数を用いた詰将棋の解法" in *コンピュータ将棋の進歩2* (Kyoritsu). The GPW'95 proceedings review (ICCA Journal, Dec 1995) describes a tsume-shogi solver using "a depth-first iterative-deepening method, based on conspiracy numbers (McAllester, 1988; Schaeffer, 1992)," with multiple iterative deepening and dynamic evaluation by hashing, which solved an **873-step** mating problem. Seo's line then became PN* (Seo, Iida & Uiterwijk, *Artificial Intelligence* 129:253–277, 2001), whose solver solved *Microcosmos*, the 1525-ply tsume problem (1997). **Relation:** historically important and often miscited: in tsume-shogi the *conspiracy* formulation was a way-station — the durable Japanese contribution was its proof-number descendants (PN*, PDS, df-pn), which is why modern Japanese tsume literature is filed under 証明数, not 共謀数.
- **Kitamura Taiji et al., 1996,** "共謀数に基づく詰将棋ゲーム木の並列探索" (parallel search of tsume-shogi game trees based on conspiracy numbers), *Record of the Tokai-Section Joint Conference of the Institutes of Electrical and Related Engineers*, p. 279 — bibliographic record only in J-GLOBAL; no abstract or results were retrieved. Flagged as existence-only.
- **Post-2015 Japanese work:** searches in Japanese (共謀数 + GPW; 共謀数 + ニューラルネットワーク/学習) returned no post-2015 GPW/IPSJ conspiracy-number papers; the living Japanese thread is the JAIST (Iida) English-language SCN/PPN programme in §4.2, whose KAKEN project pages list 共謀数 / シングル共謀数 among their keywords. No CNS use in contemporary shogi/chess/Go *playing* programs (which are alpha-beta+NNUE or AlphaZero-style) was found.
- Sources: http://www2.computer-shogi.org/gpw/gpw2_e.html (index); https://www.academia.edu/119343766/H_Matsubara_ed_Proceedings_of_the_Game_Programming_Workshop_in_Japan_951 (index); https://jglobal.jst.go.jp/detail?JGLOBAL_ID=200902158204223216 (index); http://kakinoki.o.oo7.jp/free/DoubleCount.pdf (index, citing Seo 1998); read 2026-11-20.

#### 4.5 Monte Carlo conspiracy search — what exists and what does not

Zhang's thesis (§4.2) frames the synthesis explicitly: MCTS uses information *beneath* the leaves (Monte-Carlo evaluation), CNS/PNS use information *above* the leaves (tree shape), so combining them is natural — and proposes PPNS as such a combination on the proof-number side. On the conspiracy side proper, the only MCTS combination located is Shperberg et al.'s BVPI method (§4.3). **No algorithm published under the name "Monte Carlo Conspiracy (Number) Search" was found** in the searches performed, and no CNS revival in mainstream chess/shogi/Go engines in 2020–2025 was found; the 2015–2021 revival is concentrated in Hex (SCNS), the JAIST analytic programme, and BVPI-MCTS.

### 5. Conspiracy number as a confidence score for self-play training data — negative result

**Question:** Has anyone used a search-tree robustness measure like a conspiracy number as a per-position confidence/reliability score for training data in self-play RL (AlphaZero-style pipelines)?

**Answer: No — explicit negative result within the following search scope.** Searches performed 2026-11-20 (web index, English and Japanese): `"conspiracy number" + AlphaZero / self-play / neural network training`; `"conspiracy" + search confidence + training data + self-play reinforcement learning`; `共謀数 + ニューラルネットワーク + 学習`; `"proof number" + neural network + self-play training data weighting`; plus the Athénan/Descent full texts (§3.2), which are the natural home for such a device in a minimax pipeline. No paper, thesis, or program documentation using a conspiracy number — or a proof number, or any minimax-tree leaf-flip robustness count — to annotate, weight, filter, or set targets for self-play training records was returned or found.

**Closest analogues (none is the experiment's device):**

1. **SCN as a position annotation (Zhang & Iida, §4.2)** — a conspiracy-derived scalar stored alongside a position's evaluation, used for *post-hoc analysis* of game progress. Same data shape as the proposal (position + evaluation + conspiracy scalar), different purpose: no training, no confidence weighting.
2. **Critical positions (Khalid et al., §4.2)** — CN recorded per position to *flag* positions for a different in-game treatment (speculative play). Again annotation-for-decision, not annotation-for-learning.
3. **Lorenz's difficulty use** — as summarised in the minimax-pathology literature (Luštrek & Gams line, *Theoretical Computer Science*): "Lorenz successfully used conspiracy numbers as a measure of how difficult a position is to evaluate in a chess program to lead the opponent into difficult positions." A per-position CN used *strategically at play time*, not in training.
4. **Proof Cost Network (Wu et al., §3.4)** — a proof-derived scalar is a *training target for a separate solver network*; the direction is reversed (predict the robustness/effort, don't record it as data confidence), and the downstream task is solving, not self-play improvement.
5. **Athénan's tree learning (Cohen-Solal, §3.2)** — self-play minimax training *does* consume per-tree-state information beyond the played path (all searched states, with their searched values), i.e., the pipeline already stores search-derived per-position data — but the stored quantity is the backed-up *value*, unweighted by any robustness/confidence measure.
6. **Uncertainty-guided AlphaZero (NeurIPS 2025)** — "Uncertainty-Guided Exploration for Efficient AlphaZero Training" proposes a Label Change Rate metric, branches self-play from high-uncertainty positions, and ensembles value labels, reporting +58.5% sample efficiency on 9×9 Go early in training and +47.3% on 19×19 Go late. This is the modern, MCTS-side proof that *search-derived per-position uncertainty can profitably steer self-play data generation* — but the measure is label instability across search, the intervention is branching/exploration (not a stored confidence field or loss weight), and conspiracy numbers are not involved. It is the paper the experiment's second half will most likely be compared against, and it should be cited and differentiated.

**Implication:** the annotation half of the experiment appears genuinely open. Its risk is not prior art but validation: no located work demonstrates that a leaf-flip count correlates with the *reliability of a neural backed-up value* (leaf errors under a network are correlated and magnitude-dependent, violating CNS's implicit equal-likelihood assumption — §1.6), so an empirical CN-vs-value-error calibration study is the missing piece any reviewer will ask for.

## Closest Prior Art and Main Open Gap

**Closest prior art (in order):**

1. **Pawlewicz & Hayward, SCNS (2015/2016)** — conspiracy-interval best-first leaf selection in a playing program; the experiment's selection rule is a neural-evaluator, category-threshold variant of it.
2. **Zhang & Iida, Single Conspiracy Number (2018/2019)** — a single threshold conspiracy count computed inside a conventional search and attached to a position next to its evaluation; the experiment's annotation is this, repurposed for training records (with two ±0.2 boundaries instead of one threshold).
3. **Cohen-Solal / Cohen-Solal & Cazenave, Athénan–Descent (2020/2023, arXiv:2012.10700)** — UBFM/Descent + neural leaf evaluation + self-play tree learning, with no conspiracy component; the experiment's substrate and its most likely baseline.
4. **Lorenz et al., CCNS / Cc2s (1995/2000)** — decision-change (extended) conspiracy numbers and single-fault-stable decisions; the classical evidence that conspiracy-controlled search can beat alpha-beta tactically.
5. **Shperberg, Shimony & Felner, BVPI-MCTS (2017)** — probabilistic conspiracy sets as a batch leaf-selection rule in MCTS; the only explicitly probabilistic-conspiracy search work located.
6. **Wu et al., Proof Cost Network (ICLR 2022) + Gao et al., FDFPN-CNN (IJCAI 2017)** — neural networks combined with the proof-number cousin of conspiracy numbers, for solving.

**Main open gap.** No located work (i) selects UBFM/Descent expansions by a minimal conspiracy against a value-category boundary, (ii) evaluates leaves with a self-play-trained neural network under a conspiracy rule, or (iii) stores a conspiracy/robustness number as a confidence field on self-play training records and uses it in learning. Gap (iii) is the cleanest novelty; gap (i) is an incremental-but-testable variant of SCNS; the combination — especially if CN-as-confidence is shown to be calibrated against neural value error and to improve tree learning — is, on the evidence above, unpublished.
